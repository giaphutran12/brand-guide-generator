## Do's and Don'ts

- Do use #000000 on #FFFFFF for body text on page background (21:1, WCAG AAA).
- Do use #FFFFFF on #1B2B79 for text on primary (12.59:1, WCAG AAA).
- Do use #1B2B79 on #FFFFFF for primary on page background (12.59:1, WCAG AAA).
- Do use #000000 on #FA4616 for text on vivid red accent (5.95:1, WCAG AA).
- Do (Business as Activism): Name the action Patagonia takes and the environmental interests it serves.
- Don't (Business as Activism): Substitute vague statements about caring for concrete commitments.
- Do (Collective and Mobilizing): Use we and our to frame shared stakes, then offer a specific way to participate.
- Don't (Collective and Mobilizing): Leave readers with an abstract problem and no next step.
- Do (Outdoor-Specific and Evocative): Use concrete terrain, activities and moments to describe what gear enables.
- Don't (Outdoor-Specific and Evocative): Rely on luxury language or empty claims about adventure.
- Do (Firm, Evidence-Led Advocacy): Pair policy positions with named rules, specific consequences and relevant numbers.
- Don't (Firm, Evidence-Led Advocacy): Replace reasoned opposition with insults or unsupported certainty.
- Don't use these words in copy: luxury, exclusive, effortless, planet-saving miracle, eco-friendly vibes, revolutionary, buy now!!!.

## Voice

- Use Title Case for most headings; reserve all caps for a short campaign statement like the home-page slogan.
- Aim for sentences around 19 words, allowing longer, information-dense sentences in policy advocacy.
- Favor first-person plural for commitments and shared responsibility; use second person selectively for gear and participation.
- Use declarative sentences and direct imperatives, without exclamation marks or rhetorical questions.
- Use contractions in consumer and activism copy; adopt more formal phrasing in official policy correspondence.
- Preserve precise figures, dates and policy names; expand technical acronyms on first mention in advocacy copy.
